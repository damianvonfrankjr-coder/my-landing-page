Frequently asked questions

Straight answers about what a Reserve Study is, what it costs, and how the process works. Don’t see your question? Ask us directly.

What is a Reserve Study?

A financial guide that helps community associations budget for future major repairs and replacements of common-area components — roofs, pavement, mechanical systems, and the like. Properly developed, it minimizes surprises while enhancing proactive maintenance and property values.

Why do we need one?

It projects major projects over 30 years so the community can prepare financially and avoid special assessments. It promotes proactive maintenance that enhances aesthetics and resale values, and coordinated projects can reduce costs through economies of scale. Several states — California, Florida, New Jersey, and Tennessee among them — legally require one.

How much does a Reserve Study cost?

Cost depends on scope and travel. Simple communities may run around $1,500, while complex properties can exceed $10,000. Most full studies fall between $2,500 and $5,500. Future updates typically cost 65–85% of the original with a site visit, or 25–35% without one.

How long does it take?

We aim to complete most studies within four weeks of the inspection date. Timelines can extend with property complexity, size, or delays in receiving critical information such as financial data.

What information do you need for a proposal?

Essentials: property or association name, property type, address, contact information, number of units, and general maintenance responsibilities. Helpful extras: governing documents, property maps, prior studies, construction year, building count, amenity descriptions, and any major concerns.

Are Reserve Studies legally required?

In many states, yes — California, New Jersey, Florida, Tennessee, Virginia, and Washington among them, with Illinois considering new legislation. Requirements vary by state; the Community Associations Institute maintains state-by-state reserve requirement information.

How often should we update our study?

CAI recommends a “with site visit” study at least every three years. State specifics vary — three-year intervals in California, five-year in Tennessee, ten-year structural intervals in Florida. Given economic fluctuations, annual updates are recommended for complex properties.

Is a site visit required every time?

No. Best practice is a site-visit update every three years, with interim no-site-visit updates acceptable in between. Timing depends on the age and quality of your previous study.

Who conducts a Reserve Study?

A credentialed provider — someone holding Reserve Specialist (RS) or Professional Reserve Analyst (PRA) credentials. Architects, engineers, or consultants with extensive capital planning experience may also qualify depending on their reserve knowledge.

What’s included in the report?

A component inventory with details on useful life and costs, 20–30 year financial projections, an evaluation of your current budget, and funding recommendations. Ours also includes a dynamic Excel file so your association can run “what-if” testing at its discretion.

Operating vs. reserve expenses — what’s the difference?

The operating budget covers routine annual expenses like landscaping contracts or snow removal. Reserve funds cover larger, less frequent capital projects — roof replacements, asphalt resurfacing, major equipment.

What if we don’t follow the funding recommendations?

You risk special assessments, loan dependence, and deferred maintenance — which reduce property values, raise emergency project costs, and may create legal liability. Some states require compliance or mandate disclosures about budget decisions.

What components are included?

All common-area assets are considered. Per CAI standards a component must be the association’s maintenance obligation, have reasonably anticipated replacement needs, and exceed a cost threshold (typically 0.5–1% of total budget). Common examples: paving, painting, roofing, elevators, fencing, HVAC, pools, and courts.

Will it tell us exactly when to replace things?

Yes, with context: the study indicates when the association should be financially capable of completing a project. Items may function beyond their anticipated useful life — the point is to have funds available even if replacement is delayed.

Can reserve funds cover emergency repairs?

Generally yes, if the emergency relates to reserve components and meaningfully extends remaining life. State law or governing documents may impose restrictions, so consulting legal counsel is advisable.

Can reserves fund capital improvements or new amenities?

For brand-new assets, no — reserves support repairs and replacements of existing components; spending them on new amenities undermines the funding for what they were intended to maintain. New projects are typically funded from operating surplus, loans, or special assessments. Natural evolution of an existing component — say, adding cameras to an existing surveillance system — can qualify.

How does a study prevent special assessments?

Without proper reserve planning, many associations have no choice but special assessments or loans when major projects hit. A well-prepared study communicates predictable projects clearly and structures the funding plan so surprise expenses don’t happen.

What inflation and interest assumptions are used?

Inflation assumptions typically range 2–4%, though recent inflation has run higher for many components. Historical interest earned on reserves averages around 1%, though current investment options may provide 2% or more. Consult investment specialists and accountants for your specifics.

How do we track funding after delivery?

Update reserve data and projections annually and adjust subsequent budgets accordingly — via spreadsheets, accounting software, or specialized reserve software. Every engagement of ours includes a dynamic Excel file designed to aid exactly this.

“Useful life” vs. “remaining useful life”?

Useful life is the total expected lifespan of a component when replaced under normal circumstances — a new roof lasting 20 years. Remaining useful life is how many years are left before the existing item needs replacement — an existing roof with roughly 7 years to go.

Can we update only the cost estimates?

Yes — a “no site visit” update is available if the prior report was completed within the past two years and no reserve projects have occurred. Otherwise a more significant update is necessary.

How do you estimate costs 20–30 years out?

Current cost estimates draw on the property’s project history, contractor quotes, our own experience and data, and vendor research. Within the 30-year projection, each component’s current cost is increased annually by the inflation rate stated in the report.

What happens if we underfund our reserves?

Emergency special assessments or loans, decreased owner satisfaction, negative resale impacts, difficulty with insurance or lender approvals, and state compliance issues where applicable.

Could a study affect loans or insurance?

Yes — lenders and insurance carriers increasingly request Reserve Study reports along with budgets. Having a study and budgeting appropriately demonstrates financial stability and proactive fiscal responsibility.

Can we request changes to the report?

Every engagement includes a 60-day revision period for your board, committee members, and property management to review and request changes. We ask for documentation supporting requested changes to maintain the report’s integrity.

Can future study updates be a reserve component?

Yes — under updated CAI Reserve Study Standards, professional inspections, evaluations, and related building services can qualify as reserve components when a specific completion interval is established.

What is a cost threshold?

The specific cost at which a component is considered material to the association. Items below the threshold are absorbed by the operating budget; substantial expenses above it are funded through reserves.

What happens during a site inspection?

The inspector documents all common-area components — measurements, photographs, visual condition assessments, and notes — and reviews maintenance practices and any documented repair or replacement history. This determines the remaining useful life of existing components.

How long does the inspection take?

It varies entirely with size and complexity: a small, simple HOA may take a few hours; a high-rise condominium with complex design could take two days; an expansive resort property could take more than a week.

What types of properties need one?

Any property responsible for maintaining common or shared infrastructure benefits from long-term capital planning. Reserve Studies are common for condominiums, HOAs, and townhomes; Capital Reserve Plans serve commercial, mixed-use, office, and worship properties in the same way.
